## The letter

Dear [Name],

Every children's money book on the shelf teaches earning or saving. Piggy banks. Lemonade stands. Three jars with labels. Then the kid walks into an actual store with actual birthday money and improvises.

I wrote the book for that moment, and I think there's a story in it for you.

*Clarence Gets a Bargain* follows one kid through one complete purchase, beginning to end: he wishes for a robot, earns it with a year of chores and grades, does "shopping homework" in the sale ads, compares models in the aisle, finds the markdown, remembers the coupon at the register, pays the sales tax — and watches his mom photograph the receipt before the car leaves the parking lot. That last habit is the one adults skip: the receipt is what makes returns, exchanges, and warranty claims winnable, and almost nobody teaches it to children.

I'm an attorney, so here is the claim with its qualifier attached: to our knowledge, no other narrative picture book for ages 6–10 has ever traced the full consumer arc — wish to receipt, research to protection. If a reporter finds one that beat us to it, I will read it to my kids and concede gracefully. Nobody has found one yet.

The rest of the book works the same way. Sixteen-plus financial concepts are hidden inside the plot — budgeting, comparison shopping, clearance, 529 college savings, charity, consumer awareness — and the back matter carries a 21-term glossary where every definition points back to the page where the term happens in the story. The vocabulary comes with a memory attached. Kids don't study it; they've already lived it by the time they get there.

Why this is timely for your audience:

- **The gap is real and measurable.** Thirty states now guarantee a personal finance course — in high school. Money habits form around age 7. That's roughly a decade of nothing, and it's where this book lives.
- **Kids are consumers years before anyone tells them they have rights as one.** Receipts, price accuracy, returns, warranties. A conversation nobody is having at the elementary level.
- **Seasonal hooks built in.** The book closes with Mom promising future lessons on Black Friday, Cyber Monday, and Prime Day. Holiday-shopping segments and back-to-school features write themselves.
- **The author story has teeth.** Attorney by day, mixed-media artist after hours, dad throughout. I once paid one cent for a pair of shoes that compared at $293 — a clearance sticker reading "99% Savings," and I kept the tag. Separately, I got a merchant on a cruise ship to match the sale price on his own company's website. Ask me about either on air; both hold up, and both are just comparison shopping with the receipts.

The book is endorsed by a 30-year veteran K–5 educator and by the 2026 EIFLE Educator of the Year, and it ships with a free classroom toolkit — lesson plans, assessments, a standards chart mapped to five national frameworks — so the education angle comes pre-verified.

I'm available for interviews, guest segments, written Q&As, and speaking dates for parent, teacher, and financial-education audiences. I keep answers short, I don't do jargon, and I bring the shoes story.

Thanks for reading this far. The kid gets the robot; your audience gets the reason he deserved it.

## What NOT to alter

- ⛔ **This letter previously merged two separate stories into one false claim** — that he
  "negotiated $300 shoes down to one cent on a cruise ship." He did not. The cruise-ship
  win is a website price-match with **no penny and no dollar figure on record.** The penny
  is a found Nordstrom Rack clearance sticker on shoes that compared at **$293**. See
  `reference/deal-master-facts.md`, which wins over any copy. **"Negotiated" is a banned
  verb.** $293 is $293 — never "$300."
- Keep the "to our knowledge" qualifier on the first-book claim.
- Do not reveal the Aisle Five moment or the last-name wordplay if a
  journalist asks for "the best scene" — point them to press-kit Q5's
  handling. Tease the orange stickers; guard the reveal.
