## LinkedIn 1 — The Shelf Problem (flagship category post)

Walk the money shelf in any kids' bookstore. I did, before I wrote mine.

Lemonade stands. Piggy banks. Three little jars labeled spend-save-give, where "spend" gets one page and a guilty look.

Every one of those books teaches earning or saving. And every kid who reads them will do something else entirely this weekend: spend. Birthday money, allowance, the $5 from Grandma. Spending is the first money skill a child actually uses; it's the one we skip.

Clarence Gets a Bargain starts where the other books stop — at the store. One kid, one robot, one complete purchase: sale ads at the kitchen counter, comparison shopping in Aisle Five, a marked-down model next to the shiny new ones, a 10%-off coupon at the register, and the sales tax line on the receipt.

Saving is in there too. So is the 529, the budget, the charity jar. But they ride along in the plot instead of wagging a finger from a sidebar.

To the kids it isn't a money book at all; it's a robot story with a heist-level deal at the end.

Ages 6–10. 36 pages. 16+ concepts that snuck in without a worksheet.

## LinkedIn 2 — Ready for Saturday, Not for 65

Financial literacy for kids has a timing problem.

Most of it aims at a horizon no child can see: compound interest, credit scores, retirement. All real. All decades out. Meanwhile the kid is eight, holding $20 of birthday money, standing in a store that spent millions figuring out how to get it.

We're preparing children for 65 and sending them to the register unarmed today.

Money habits are largely set by age 7. Thirty states now guarantee a personal finance course — in high school, roughly ten years after the cement dries.

So flip the order. Teach spending first:

1. What a sale ad actually says
2. How to compare two prices on the same shelf
3. Why the orange-sticker aisle exists
5. What that extra line on the receipt is (hello, sales tax)

Yes, I skipped 4. It was about coupons. Kids who read the book don't skip the coupon.

A child who can walk into a store and come out with the smarter deal has a working money skill by Friday. The 401(k) talk can wait for a learner's permit.

That's the premise of Clarence Gets a Bargain — one kid, one robot, one real purchase from ad to receipt. Story first; money smarts sneak in.

## LinkedIn 3 — The Attorney's Case for Little Consumers

I'm an attorney. I read fine print for a living. Here is what almost nobody teaches kids: they are already consumers.

By age ten, a kid has been part of hundreds of transactions. Nobody has told them what a receipt is for. That it's proof. That the return, the exchange, the warranty claim, the price adjustment — all of it runs through the little strip of paper getting crumpled in a pocket right now.

Consumer education is the missing wing of kids' financial literacy. We teach earning (fine) and saving (also fine) and never mention that the buyer has rights, the sticker price isn't always the final price, and "newest" is a marketing word, not a measurement.

In Clarence Gets a Bargain, the mom photographs the receipt the second she sits down in the car. Every time. Readers ask whether that detail comes from my house. No comment.

My credentials, for the record: I once paid one cent for a pair of shoes that compared at $293. The sticker said "99% Savings." I kept the tag. On a separate trip I got a merchant on a cruise ship to match the sale price on his own company's website, because I knew their pricing better than the clerk did. Neither one is a negotiating story. Both are comparison shopping.

Raise kids who ask. Raise kids who keep the receipt.

## Facebook 4 — Steal the Game

Steal this game from page 27 of my book:

Next grocery run, everyone hunts for the biggest savings. Best deal wins. Loser carries the bags in from the car.
