# Parenting Organizations, Media, and Special-Population Groups

Checked July 2026. Same rules as the other target files: where a group is too young, defunct,
or a genuine stretch, it goes on the cut list instead of padding the page.

**Read this first, because it changes how you write to all of them.**

Parenting organizations almost never buy books. They *recommend* books, *review* books, or
*hand out* books somebody else paid for. So there are only two payoffs in this whole
document, and they need different letters:

1. **Reach** — a review, an award seal, a column mention, a webinar slot. Costs a copy and
   a stamp. Pays in traffic.
2. **Volume** — a program partner buys a few hundred copies because a funder is paying.
   Slow, and worth more than every review combined.

Do not send the school letter to these people. A principal cares about a standards
crosswalk. A parenting editor cares about whether the thing photographs well in a gift
guide, and a program director cares about whether a grant will cover it.

# LANE A — Parenting media 🔑

Fastest payoff on this page, and the one nobody in kids' financial literacy is working.

### Parenting Media Association
`parentmedia.org`

A national trade association of regional parenting media companies — magazines, websites,
newsletters, and local family events across the US, Canada, and Australia. Combined reach
they claim is **over six million readers a month.** Annual convention and awards each

work with the members.

Two ways in. Work the member directory city by city, or join as an associate and get at the
whole room at once. The directory is free and immediate; start there.

# LANE B — Parenting awards that still exist

### NAPPA — National Parenting Product Awards
**Elena Epstein**, Director · `elena.epstein@nappaawards.com` · `nappaawards.com`

- **Children's Books** and **Parenting Books** are both standing categories
- Entries accepted **year-round** — no deadline to miss
- Books from **any release year** are eligible, so being published already is not a problem
- **Self-published titles are accepted** — no restriction anywhere in their rules
- Two physical samples; testing runs six to eight weeks
- Submit the form first, then email Elena for the shipping address

On money: their FAQ says NAPPA "is not a 'Pay-to-Play' program" and separately that entry
fees are "among the most reasonable submission fees around." Both things are true at once —
there is a fee, it is not large, and the award is not bought. **Email Elena and ask the

Best award fit on this page. Real reach with parents, open to indies, no deadline pressure.

### The pay-to-enter tier
Mom's Choice Awards, Family Choice Awards, Tillywig, Creative Child, Dr. Toy. All accept
self-published titles. All charge. All produce a seal.

Honest read: parents recognize the Mom's Choice seal and it does no harm on a cover. None of
them will impress a librarian or a district. Pick one, at most, and spend the rest on
postage.

### ⚠️ Parents' Choice Awards — gone
**The Parents' Choice Foundation closed on July 31, 2022.** It still appears on nearly every
"awards for children's books" listicle online, because those lists are never updated. If
anyone advises you to enter it, they are reading a stale page.

# LANE C — Parent educators, as a profession

These are people who teach parenting for a living. They do not buy in volume. They put
things on reading lists and in front of rooms.

### NPEN — National Parenting Education Network 🔑
`npen.org`

Thirty years advancing science-based parenting education. They run a conference **and a
monthly professional development webinar series.**

That webinar series is the target, not the conference. A monthly slot needs filling twelve
times a year, and "what nobody teaches children about spending money" is a session, not an
ad. Free to them, and you are talking to the people who train the people who teach parents.

### NCFR — National Council on Family Relations
`ncfr.org`

Founded 1938. Owns the **Certified Family Life Educator (CFLE)** credential — the national
standard in family life education — publishes the field's journals, and runs an annual
conference. NCFR and NPEN work together closely, so an introduction at one carries at the
other.

Family resource management is an actual CFLE content area. This is not a stretch fit.

### National Center for Families Learning
Family literacy at national scale, Louisville-based. Their whole model is a parent and a
child learning the same thing at the same time, which is exactly how a 36-page read-aloud
gets used at a kitchen table.

### National PTA / Maryland PTA / Delaware PTA
Already in `organizations-target-list.md` under youth organizations. Worth naming again here
because a PTA's money is not a school's money — a PTA-funded classroom set never touches a
district budget.

# LANE D — ADHD 🔑🔑 — and this one is in Baltimore

You asked about ADHD specifically. It is the strongest special-population lane on this page,
by a distance, and the reason is not sympathy — it is that the topic is already theirs.

### The argument, before the contacts
CHADD's own published material states that **more than 60 percent of adults with ADHD have
significant to severe financial difficulties.** They have run articles titled *"How Can You
Teach Kids with ADHD to Manage Money?"* and *"Helping Teens Avoid the Pitfalls in
Spending."* ADDitude has run *"Behavioral Finance for Kids with ADHD."*

So the field has already decided that impulsive spending is an ADHD parenting problem. What
it does not have is a picture book where the entire plot is a kid **stopping** in front of a
purchase — looking at two things, doing the arithmetic, waiting, and choosing the cheaper
one on his own. That is a self-regulation story wearing a robot costume, and you did not
write it that way on purpose, which is exactly why it will read as honest.

Do not medicalize the pitch. Say the book is about a kid who pauses, and let them make the
connection. They will make it faster than you can.

### CHADD
`chadd.org` · publishes *Attention* magazine, runs the National Resource Center on ADHD, and
has local chapters including in Maryland. Chapter meetings are parent audiences who show up
voluntarily on a weeknight — the most receptive room a children's author can walk into.

### ADDitude Magazine
`submissions@additudemag.com`

They take **blog posts from parents**, not press releases. Their guidelines ask for a 500-
to 800-word sample post on the topic you want to write about, plus a sample of previously
published work.

**Write the post, not the pitch.** Something like: a lawyer and a dad on why he wrote a
children's book about pausing in a store aisle. The book appears in the byline. That is the
entire mechanism, and it is free.

They also warn they cannot reply to every submission. Send it and move on.

### Understood.org
Learning and thinking differences, enormous parent readership, and they publish practical
skills content rather than clinical material.

# LANE F — Military families 🔑

Funded, organized, and already required to care about money.

### Military Child Education Coalition
`militarychild.org`

Supports **1.65 million military-connected children.** Runs the **National Training
Seminar** — a national event for educators, youth-serving professionals, military family
members, and leaders. Foundation and corporate partners fund the resources MCEC produces,
which is the important part: somebody else pays.

### Why the fit is unusually good
Financial readiness is not optional in the military — it is a command interest and a funded
program. Military kids also move every two or three years, which means a new store, a new
town, and a parent explaining money again from scratch. A single 36-page book that travels
in a footlocker does something a curriculum binder cannot.

Also worth naming: **National Military Family Association**, **Blue Star Families**, and
**Military Saves** (the DoD-aligned campaign). And every installation has a school liaison
officer whose job is exactly this kind of introduction.

# LANE G — Fathers and grandfamilies

### National Fatherhood Initiative
`fatherhood.org` · `store.fatherhood.org`

Runs **24:7 Dad®**, **InsideOut Dad®**, and **Understanding Dad™** — curricula delivered
through community organizations, corrections programs, and social services. Their five
domains include parenting skills and self-care.

You are a dad who wrote the book after watching his own kid, and the book's whole premise is
a parent teaching a child something in a car and a store aisle rather than at a desk. NFI
sells curriculum through a store; a companion picture book is an adjacent product, not a
competing one.

### Generations United
`gu.org` · National Center on Grandfamilies · Grandfamilies & Kinship Support Network ·
Global Intergenerational Conference

Grandparents and relatives raising children, very often on fixed incomes. For those
households the money conversation is not enrichment — it is Tuesday. And the intergenerational
frame suits a read-aloud better than almost any other family structure, because the adult
reading it has sixty years of stories about prices.

### AARP
Grandparenting content and financial literacy content, both already in-house, both already
enormous. The grandparent-buying-a-gift-that-teaches-something angle is theirs to run.

# LANE H — Divorce and family courts — the honest answer

You raised children of divorce. Here is the straight read, because padding this would waste
your postage.

**AFCC — Association of Family and Conciliation Courts** is the interdisciplinary
association of family court judges, mediators, custody evaluators, and family law attorneys.
Your law credential would make you credible in that room in a way no other children's author
is. Nobody else on that list drafts contracts for a living.

**But the book doesn't fit.** *Clarence Gets a Bargain* has an intact two-parent household,
a mom in the store and a dad at home, and no separation content of any kind. The honest
connection is the co-parenting money conflict — different rules at each house, one parent
buying to compensate — and that is a *different book*, not a different pitch for this one.

If this lane interests you, it is a book two conversation. Do not spend money on it now.

Same logic, briefly, for the rest of the special-population list: foster and kinship care
overlaps with Generations United above; **Boys & Girls Clubs' Money Matters** is a teen
program; faith-based family ministries buy a lot of books but you would be writing to a
market you do not know.
