# CGB Disruptive Campaign Playbook — Six Ways to Stop the Scroll

The competitive research came back and it's worse than expected. The entire kids'
money-book category markets itself the same three ways: a pastel pig graphic, a
plea to get on a listicle, and a credit union storytime during "Smart Money Week."
ALA's flagship program for this category is called — real name — "Cents-ational
Reads." The most-marketed book in the space (Berenstain Bears, 260 million copies)
promotes itself with free-book giveaways at library events. That's the ceiling.

Nobody markets a kids' money book like a store. The book's whole conceit is retail.
Sale ads, clearance stickers, coupons, receipts, a register. Every one of these
campaigns weaponizes a piece of the retail experience that people already have a
Pavlovian response to. You don't have to teach anyone to open a coupon flyer or
read a receipt. They already do it on autopilot. We just hide the book inside.

One rule governs all six: the campaign asset should not look like book marketing.
It should look like retail. The double-take IS the hook.

---

## Campaign 1 — The Sea-Mart Weekly Circular

**The asset:** A real, printed, full-color grocery-store sale flyer. Newsprint
stock. Screaming price bursts. BOGO banners. Produce-aisle layout. Twelve fake
products with real prices ("RoBimmie 3000 — WAS $89.99"). And buried on page 2,
between the marked-down robots and the pudding, one item that's an actual book.

**Why it works:** Nobody throws out a coupon flyer without scanning it. It's the
single most-opened piece of unsolicited print in America. A media kit gets binned
by the front office; a sale circular gets read at the kitchen counter — which is
page 4 of the book, happening in real life, in the reader's hands.

**Where it goes:** School front offices, pediatrician waiting rooms, credit union
lobbies, teacher mailboxes at conferences, tucked into every review copy shipped.

**The hook inside:** A clip-out coupon (dashed border, scissors icon — the site's
whole design language already) with a QR code that lands on the Receipt Builder,
not the homepage. Let the kid play cashier first. The buy button finds them later.

**The digital twin:** The flipbook magazine already on the site IS this circular.
Post single "products" from it as social tiles — a sale-ad burst for "COMPARISON
SHOPPING: 100% OFF" looks like retail spam until you read it, and that beat of
confusion is the engagement.

**Cost:** Print run of 500 circulars ≈ the price of one conference ad nobody reads.

## Campaign 2 — The Piggy Bank Retirement Party

**The asset:** A mock press release + social series announcing that the piggy bank
is retiring after 400 years of loyal service. Gold watch. Farewell sheet cake.
A somber portrait with a "THANK YOU FOR YOUR SERVICE" banner.

The copy picks the fight the whole brand is built on:

> After four centuries teaching kids to put money IN, the piggy bank is stepping
> down. It never taught a single kid how to take money OUT without getting fleeced.
> It never read a sale ad. It never checked a price. It has never once paid sales
> tax. We wish it a restful retirement. The spending years start now.

**Why it works:** Every competitor worships the pig. The Money Savvy Pig has four
slots; the Moneybunnies hoard carrots; Berenstain cubs run lemonade stands. Roasting
the category's sacred mascot — affectionately — is instant pattern-break. Parenting
social media runs on gentle heresy. This is shareable disagreement, and every share
carries the thesis: saving books teach half the job.

**The receipts (literally):** Every post ends with the fairness note that already
lives on the comparison page — those books are essential; this one teaches the part
that comes first — and links to vs-other-money-books.html. The attack ad funnels
into the most gracious page on the site. That whiplash is the brand.

**Formats:** Instagram carousel (retirement announcement → career highlights →
"what the pig never taught"), a printable "retirement card" teachers can put on
the classroom piggy bank, a 30-second video of an actual piggy bank being handed
a tiny gold watch.

## Campaign 3 — The Receipt

**The asset:** A three-foot-long pharmacy-style receipt. Thermal paper texture,
monospace font, itemized to the penny:

```
SEA-MART  #0022        AISLE 5
--------------------------------
READS SALE ADS           $0.00
CHECKS PRICE STICKERS    $0.00
SPOTS A MARKDOWN         $0.00
HANDS COUPON TO CASHIER  $0.00
UNDERSTANDS SALES TAX    $0.00
IMMUNE TO "NEWER=BETTER" $0.00
--------------------------------
SUBTOTAL:            ONE STORY
YOU SAVED:  EVERY TOY-AISLE
            MELTDOWN, FOREVER
--------------------------------
ITEM 979-8-234-07638-0  $19.99
CUSTOMER COPY — KEEP FOREVER
```

**Why it works:** Receipt paper is the one print format with zero advertising on
it, ever. A receipt that isn't a receipt gets picked up, read to the bottom, and
photographed. The absurd length is the thumb-stopper; the $0.00 column is the
pitch; the exact ISBN at the bottom is the wink for the librarians.

**Formats:** Bookmarks printed on actual thermal stock (costs pennies, feels like
an object from the book's universe). A vertical social tile — receipts are already
a native Instagram format. A six-foot vinyl version hanging at any conference booth.
Slip one into every book shipped: the packing slip IS the ad.

**The tie-in:** QR at the receipt's bottom edge — where the survey code goes on a
real receipt — to the Receipt Builder, where kids print their own.

## Campaign 4 — Aisle Five Exists (the orange sticker takeover)

**The asset:** Sheets of fire-engine-red, neon-orange, and highlighter-yellow
clearance stickers — the exact palette Mom names on page 16 — that say things like:

- `MARKED DOWN: boring money worksheets — WAS: your kid's attention  NOW: $0.00`
- `CLEARANCE: "newer is always better" — everything must go`
- `MANAGER'S SPECIAL: one very determined kid, aisle five`

**Why it works:** The orange clearance sticker is the strongest conditioned stimulus
in retail. Eyes snap to it involuntarily. Putting that trigger on things that aren't
merchandise — a conference badge, a teacher-toolkit folder, the book itself — creates
the double-take, and the small print rewards whoever leans in.

**The booth version:** At any conference, build the table as a clearance endcap.
Big orange AISLE 5 sign overhead. Everything on the table wears a markdown sticker.
Attendees have walked past forty polite tablecloth booths; they have never walked
past a clearance bin without slowing down. The sign says "EVERYTHING AT THIS TABLE
HAS A STICKER. ASK US WHY." — and the answer is the elevator pitch.

**Spoiler discipline:** This campaign lives entirely on clearance-as-concept and the
orange-sticker aisle. It teases toward pages 13–15 and never, ever reveals what the
sign says or what Clarence thinks it says. The sticker is the trailer, not the scene.

## Campaign 5 — Guess the Price (the UGC engine)

**The asset:** A challenge lifted straight off page 28, where the Wyze family plays
it after dinner. Parents film their kid guessing what real things cost. That's it.
That's the whole format.

**Why it works:** Kids' price guesses are unhinged. A gallon of milk: "$45." Dad's
car: "$100." A Nintendo Switch: "six dollars." The reveal face is the content. It
needs no production value, no script, no budget — and every single video, from any
family, anywhere, is a 20-second argument that kids have no idea what anything
costs. Which is the book's entire reason to exist.

**The mechanics:** #GuessThePrice. A printable score card on the site (loser carries
the groceries in — page 27 rules, cite the book). A monthly "closest guess" feature
on CGB's own channels. Seed it with Clarence's own guesses from the book's world.

**The CTA that doesn't feel like one:** "There's a whole book about a kid who
learned to stop guessing." No hard sell inside the trend; the trend IS the sell.

**Why competitors can't copy it:** Their books are about earning and saving —
there's no game in a piggy bank. Price is CGB's home turf.

## Campaign 6 — The Book That Is Never On Sale

**The asset:** A pricing stunt disguised as a principle. The book about bargains
costs $19.99, every day, everywhere, no exceptions — and the site says so, proudly:

> We wrote the book on markdowns. That's exactly why we don't do them.
> A price that's fake-high on Tuesday so it can be fake-low on Friday is the
> first trick this book teaches kids to see through.

**Except.** One day a year: **Clearance Day.** Twenty-four hours. Orange takeover
of the whole site. Real markdown, real sticker, countdown clock, and a coupon —
because on page 22 the coupon stacks on the clearance price, and the store should
work like the book. Email list is the only way to know the date.

**Why it works:** Every self-published author is drowning in perpetual 99-cent
promos that scream desperation. A book that refuses to discount is a book that
believes its own price — and it converts the discount everyone else gives away
daily into one scarce annual event that builds a mailing list all year. Scarcity,
consistency, and curriculum, all in one pricing policy.

**The teach:** Clearance Day itself becomes content. Kids watch a real markdown
happen, with the real math shown on the site. The promotion is a lesson; the
lesson is a promotion. No other book in the category can even attempt this.

## Bonus — The Missing Poster

Milk-carton / telephone-pole format, stapled-flyer aesthetic, tear-off tabs:

> **MISSING: THE LESSON BETWEEN THE PIGGY BANK AND THE PAYCHECK.**
> Last seen: nowhere. Every kids' money book teaches earning it or saving it.
> The part where they actually SPEND it — at a register, on a Saturday, with a
> parent sweating the total — has been missing for decades.
> IF FOUND: aisle five. REWARD: $19.99, well spent.

Each tear-off tab is a mini coupon with the URL. Post the physical version on
community boards (libraries allow it; that's the audience). Post the digital
version once and let the parenting internet do the stapling.

---

## Sequencing — don't fire all six at once

1. **Now:** The Receipt (cheapest, fastest, pure social) + Guess the Price (starts
   the UGC flywheel; it compounds, so plant it early).
2. **Next 30 days:** Piggy Bank Retirement (needs the comparison page traffic-ready,
   which it is) + orange sticker sheets ordered for the next conference.
3. **Next 90 days:** Sea-Mart Circular print run, timed to back-to-school when
   school front offices restock their literature racks.
4. **Annual anchor:** Announce the never-on-sale policy now; hold Clearance Day
   when the email list can feel it — Black Friday week is the on-theme choice,
   since Mom names it on page 32 on her way out the door.

## What every campaign shares

- Looks like retail, not like book marketing. The double-take is the hook.
- Funnels to a *playable* page (Receipt Builder, quiz, comparison page) before the
  buy button. Sucked-in beats sold-to.
- Punches at the category ("half the job") while staying gracious to the books
  themselves — the fairness note is load-bearing everywhere.
- Never spoils the sign. Never explains the last name. The stickers, the aisle,
  the orange — all trailer, no scene.
