For the Pros

Why Clarence Belongs in Your Curriculum

Financial literacy is usually treated as a “high school” topic, but research shows that money habits are largely formed by age seven1. Most educational books on this subject are either too dry for a first-grader or too simplistic for a fifth-grader.

Clarence Gets a Bargain was built for that gap. 36 pages. Grades 1–5. The book itself is the lesson plan. What’s inside:

  • Behavioral Economics: The Wants vs. Needs decision, with real stakes a seven-year-old can feel.
  • Real-World Math: Percentage markdowns, sale pricing, and coupon savings applied at a register, not on a worksheet.
  • College Savings: A 529 account explained in a way a seven-year-old actually follows — and doesn’t roll their eyes at.
  • The Financial Glossary: Two pages of page-referenced definitions. That is what makes the book defensible as curriculum, not just a picture book.

Stop teaching money with abstract worksheets. Teach it with a mission.

Every Other Money Book Stops at the Piggy Bank.

Walk the kids’ money shelf and you’ll meet the same three books over and over. The jar book. The glossary with a mascot. The lemonade stand. All fine. All parked on the earning-and-saving side of the register. Clarence Gets a Bargain works the other side — the side your kid actually visits every week. He gets shopping homework, reads the sale ads, compares two robots in the aisle, finds the markdown, and hands the cashier a coupon. Spending well is the first money skill a kid ever uses. It shouldn’t be the last one anyone teaches.

About Jonathan Bach

Attorney. Mixed-media artist. Certified Nerd Herd member. Simultaneously right-brained and left-brained — which means he can draft a contract and illustrate a children’s book in the same afternoon.

What qualifies him to write a financial literacy book? He once got a merchant on a cruise ship to match the sale price on the company’s own website. He has also paid one cent — twice, at the same Nordstrom Rack — for a $293 pair of shoes and a $69.50 shirt. He kept both tags:Both stickers say “99% Savings.” A penny off $293 is 99.997%. Through relentless pre- and post-internet research, he has earned the reputation as a “deal master.”

He wrote Clarence Gets a Bargain because money habits form by age seven, and most books on the subject are either too dry for a first grader or too thin for a fifth grader. He fixed that. Written to plant a tiny voice in a little noggin — the one that says “Wait… is there a better deal?” — before that noggin becomes a know-it-all tween.
