# Replies received — and what went back

Tracks real responses from the outreach, what they revealed, and the reply sent. Not
every reply needs a log entry — only ones that changed something (a letter, a fact, a
process) or need a record of what was said.

---

## 1. Stacey J. Aaronson — 7 Aug 2026

**What she was replying to:** the letter in `letters-top25-week1.md` §23 / `SEND-QUEUE.md`,
which asked her — a book designer and production editor — for an unglamorous read on
whether the object "reads as professionally published or as a very determined amateur,"
and closed with "I would rather hear it from you now than infer it from a bookseller
later." That line is the bug: it told her, directly, that the book hasn't reached
booksellers yet.

**Her reply, in full:**

```
Thank you for your trust in reaching out! Writing and publishing a book can be a very
humbling experience, and I admire you for seeking a professional opinion before moving
forward. I'm happy to help!

I certainly appreciate what you're aiming to teach children about spending. It's a great
message! And I also want to preface my comments by saying that while I've done over 100
books, this is a subjective topic in many regards, so please don't take my opinion as the
one you stand on.

I read through your sample, and while I appreciate the work you put into it, there are
some elements for me that gave me pause.

For one, I'm not a huge fan of these kinds of AI images for children's books. That may
just be my opinion, but kids books lose something for me with this style of imagery. I'm
sorry to be blunt about that! It's just not my preference for illustration.

Two, dialogue is typically written in separate lines. Having it all bunched into a box
isn't ideal. I would work on utilizing the page space a bit differently, so that the
dialogue is clearer between parties. Also, I would suggest some editorial work to improve
some of the writing.

On the trim size, it's probably the best choice for you, as you're thinking of an adult
reading and holding it up. That's good!

The colored boxes give the pages interest. Those are fun! I would just consider paying a
book designer to do better for you with the text.

I definitely don't want to sound discouraging! But I would recommend some professional
attention on your book before you publish it. You don't want to publish and later try to
make it better. You want the first launch to be golden!

If you have further questions, please let me know. And if you're interested in possibly
working with me, I have an extensive website that outlines all of my services, and much
more.

Wishing you the best on your book journey!
Stacey
```

**What it confirmed:** the "AI-cover accusation" flagged as a known risk in the letter's
own internal note (§23: "Given the AI-cover accusation, her read on the art and the
object is separately useful") is real and recurring — a second professional read the art
as AI-generated. And the published-status gap isn't theoretical; it produced exactly the
"before you publish" response predicted.

**Fixed as a result:**
1. The letter itself — `letters-top25-week1.md` §23 and its `SEND-QUEUE.md` copy — now
   states the book is already printed and on sale, and that the interior was done by
   hand, not left to be inferred.
2. This reply, below.

### The reply sent

```
Stacey,

Appreciate the direct read.

One thing I should've said upfront: the book's already published — hardbound, printed, on sale now. Not a manuscript, not a proof. So "before you publish" doesn't quite apply; anything here is for whatever comes next, not a fix on this one.

Also worth knowing: a professional graphic designer built the speech boxes, fonts and layout, and two veteran teachers plus my agent worked the manuscript edits before it went to print. So it's not unreviewed — your read's just a different call than theirs, which happens. And I appreciate all the constructive criticism that I can get!

Thanks for the time on it.

Jonathan

P.S. I've used em dashes as long as I can remember and I'm not about to stop now because somebody might accuse me of being AI.
```

**Why the design/editorial credential made the cut and the art defense didn't:** the design
and editorial note corrects a factual assumption she stated as fact ("pay a book designer,"
"some professional attention") — one line, not a list, not defensive. The art question was a
taste opinion Jonathan already closed by ruling; re-litigating taste is a different move than
correcting a fact, and only the fact belongs in the reply.

**Why it's shaped this way:** thanks first, correct the one factual gap that mattered
(published status) without arguing the subjective calls (dialogue formatting, whether to
hire a designer), and close the loop instead of opening a sales conversation — she pitched
her own services at the end of her note; this reply doesn't take the bait either way, which
leaves it open without committing to anything.
