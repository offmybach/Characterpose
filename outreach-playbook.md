# Outreach Playbook — Credibility Without Amazon

Built July 2026, off the back of `competitive-audit-2026-07-seo-and-social.md`.

The decision to skip Amazon for now is workable. For the buyers who matter here —
librarians, districts, credit unions, curriculum committees — Amazon rank is not
the credential. Standards alignment, a librarian-vetted list, and an award seal
are. Those are all reachable without a KDP account.

One thing to know going in: **most of the affiliate listicles from the audit are
Amazon Associates sites.** No Amazon link, no commission, no listing. That's not
snobbery, it's their business model. So the affiliate tier is mostly closed until
you change your mind, and this playbook routes around it entirely.

The good news is the institutional tier ranks better anyway and lasts longer. An
ABA Foundation listing doesn't churn every time somebody refreshes a blog post.

Verification note: where a contact or criterion is stated below, I confirmed it
from the organization's own page. Where I couldn't confirm submission mechanics,
it says so.

## Tier 1 — Free, high authority, and you already qualify

These are the ones to do first. All five are the sort of page that the audit
found ranking on page one for category searches.

### 1. Jump$tart Clearinghouse — do this one today

The national searchable database of vetted financial education resources.

- **Why you're a fit:** listings must align with the National Standards for
  Personal Financial Education. You're already built against Jump$tart standards
  and you have the crosswalk to prove it.
- **The one catch:** resources "may not be used to sell or promote financial
  products and services." A picture book isn't a financial product, but submit
  the **free educator toolkit** as the resource and let the book ride along. That
  sails through the criterion instead of testing it.
- **Named contact:** Suzann Knight, Director of Curriculum Resources and Online
  Education — suzann.knight@jumpstart.org
- **Submit:** jumpstartclearinghouse.org

### 2. CFPB "Money as You Grow" Bookshelf

Federal government booklist of children's money books, ages 4–10. Dead center of
your audience, and it carries a .gov domain.

- **Selection:** titles were chosen by the University of Wisconsin-Extension
  Family Living Programs and the UW-Madison Center for Financial Security, based
  on the financial concepts covered and age level.
- **Caveat:** I could not find a public submission form. The route is academic,
  not bureaucratic — approach the UW-Madison Center for Financial Security
  directly about the concept coverage.
- **Your argument:** the existing shelf is heavy on saving and earning. You cover
  the transaction itself, including sales tax, which almost nothing on that shelf
  does.

### 3. ALA / FINRA Foundation — "Thinking Money for ALL Kids"

40 titles chosen by a team of librarian advisors, organized by Picture Books,
Younger Readers, Middle Grades, and YA. Free PDF, widely circulated among library
workers, and it's also listed in the Jump$tart Clearinghouse.

- **Note:** the current edition dates to 2022, so pitch it as a candidate for the
  next refresh rather than an open call.
- **Angle:** it was built to "eschew stereotypes and embrace diversity" in money
  stories. Yours features a working family talking honestly about bills and
  tradeoffs, which is a real gap on most finance shelves.

### 4. ABA Foundation Financial Literacy Book List

25 titles across K–2, 3–5, and 6–8. The audit found it ranking for category
queries, and it's bank-distributed, which puts it in front of exactly the CRA and
credit-union buyers in your institutional lane.

- **The gap to exploit:** I read all 25. **Every one teaches earning, saving, or
  investing. Not one walks a kid through a complete purchase.** Say that plainly
  in the pitch.

### 5. Council for Economic Education / EconEdLink

You're already aligned to CEE standards. Their resource library is teacher-facing
and heavily trafficked in exactly the K–5 window you're targeting.

## Tier 2 — Distribution and catalog infrastructure

This is the part that actually replaces Amazon. It's also what makes Tier 1 easy,
because most lists want a title librarians can order.

**6. IngramSpark / Ingram.** The single most important non-Amazon move. Ingram
feeds 40,000+ retailers, libraries, and schools. Without it, a librarian who wants
your book has to cut a manual PO, and most won't. This is the difference between
"interesting book" and "orderable book."

**7. Bookshop.org.** Runs off Ingram, so it comes nearly free once #6 is done.
Indie-bookstore-aligned, which reads well next to a direct-sales stance.

**8. Google Books Partner Center.** Free, and it feeds the Book entity Google is
currently missing. Directly relevant to the missing rich result.

**9. Goodreads author program.** Free, and independent of selling on Amazon. Also
the fastest way to stop "Jonathan Bach" resolving to the *Above the Clouds*
author, since it'll create a distinct author record tied to this title.

**10. WorldCat / OCLC and LibraryThing.** Library-catalog presence. You already
have an LCCN, so the cataloging groundwork is done.

**11. Edelweiss+, or Covered.** Digital catalog and galley services for
booksellers, librarians, and media. Covered launched as a cheaper alternative to
Edelweiss and NetGalley, partly to fill the gap left by Baker & Taylor's closure.
Worth pricing both.

**12. NetGalley.** Where librarians and educators leave the reviews that other
librarians read. Costly for one title; consider a co-op listing.

## Tier 3 — Reviews librarians actually trust

Collection-development librarians look for these by name. Paid, but they're the
credential that unlocks Tier 1 lists.

- **Kirkus Indie** — the heavyweight. A star here changes the conversation.
- **Foreword Clarion** — indie-friendly, well regarded, cheaper than Kirkus.
- **BlueInk Review** — founded for indie titles, respected by librarians.
- **School Library Journal** — hardest to get, worth the most for K–5.
- **Publishers Weekly BookLife** — free submission path into PW's indie channel.
- **Story Monsters Ink** — children's-specific, educator readership.

## Tier 4 — Awards (seals travel further than posts)

**EIFLE Awards — Institute for Financial Literacy. Start here.**

They run a **Children's Books of the Year** category with General and Saving
subcategories. 2026 winners included *Ten Dollar Adventure* and *The Adventure of
Money*. This is the most on-target award in existence for this book.

**And you have a warm path.** Wally Luckeydoo, who already endorsed the book, is
the **2026 EIFLE Educator of the Year**. He knows the organization, the judges,
and the calendar. Ask him to make an introduction rather than submitting cold.

Then, in rough order of value for a K–5 indie picture book: Moonbeam Children's
Book Awards, Independent Publisher (IPPY), Next Generation Indie Book Awards,
Mom's Choice Awards, Benjamin Franklin Awards (IBPA).

## Two posts to run

From the audit: the only post in this category that beat its follower count was
Tom Henske's "Everything Your Child Should Know About Money" at 22 comments on
~3K followers. It worked because it made an arrogant promise, delivered a
checklist, and left something to argue with. Both of these copy that shape.

Neither reveals the Aisle Five moment or explains the Wyze pun. Keep it that way.

### Post 1 — LinkedIn, the checklist

> Everything a fourth grader should know before you let them hold the money.
>
> 1. The price on the sticker is not the price you pay. Sales tax is coming and
> nobody warns you.
> 2. A sale price and a clearance price are two different animals.
> 3. Newer does not mean better. Sometimes newer means there's an antenna and
> nothing else.
> 4. Coupons expire. In a pocket. On the way to the store.
> 5. Read the ad before you go, not in the aisle.
> 6. "I want it" and "I need it" use the same voice. That's the trap.
> 8. If you can't say what you're giving up to buy it, you're not ready to buy it.
>
> I skipped 7. Tell me what belongs there.
>
> My 10-year-old reader put it better than I did: it "made me feel more grown-up
> and smart." That's the whole job.

*(The skipped 7 is deliberate. It's the ask, and it's the reason people comment.
Don't let anyone tidy it.)*

### Post 2 — Facebook, for parent groups and teacher groups

> Nine things my mom did that I didn't understand until I had a kid.
>
> She photographed every receipt before she pulled out of the parking space. Every
> time. I thought it was a tic. It's leverage; lose the receipt and you lose the
> argument.
>
> She read the Sunday ad inserts like a briefing.
>
> She made me guess prices at dinner. Whoever got closest to what she actually paid
> won nothing at all, and we competed like it was money.
>
> She carried the coupons and made me hand them to the cashier myself.
>
> She said no to things we could afford, which confused me for about twenty years.
>
> That's five. I put the rest in a book, because my kid wasn't going to sit still
> for the lecture and neither did I.
>
> What did yours do? I'm collecting these.

*(Note the count mismatch: nine promised, five delivered, rest handed to the
comments. Same mechanism as the missing 7. It's not sloppiness, it's the hook.)*

## Sequence

Do it in this order. Each step makes the next one easier.

1. **Ingram** (#6). Nothing in Tier 1 lands well until librarians can order it.
2. **Goodreads and Google Books** (#8, #9). Free, same week, and they start
   repairing the author-entity confusion.
3. **Jump$tart Clearinghouse** (#1). You already meet the criteria. Named contact.
4. **Wally → EIFLE** (Tier 4). Warm intro, on-target category, seal by next cycle.
5. **ABA Foundation and ALA/FINRA** (#3, #4). Pitch A/B/C above.
6. **One paid review** — Foreword Clarion or Kirkus Indie. This is what Tier 1 and
   Tier 3 both lean on.
7. **Post 1 and Post 2.** Run them a week apart, not the same day.
