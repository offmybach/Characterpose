# 5. Honest audit — where CGB passes, and the three places it can trip

### Resource type — you fit more than one box

**Lessons/Lesson Plans and Activities**, **Assessments/Quizzes**, **Games and Simulations**
(the Sea-Mart receipt builder), **Graphics** (the printable price tags, sort cards, and

### Clean passes
| # | Criterion | Read |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Focus entirely or predominantly on personal finance | 16+ concepts, 21-term glossary, the plot is a transaction |
| 3 | Created or updated within past five years | 2026 |

| 5 | Balanced and unbiased | No product, no provider, no account is recommended |
| 6 | Appropriate for target audience | Ages 6–10, grades 1–5, built with a K–5 teacher of 30+ years |

### ⚠️ Criterion 2 — name their standard exactly
"Content consistent with the **National Standards for Personal Financial Education**."

That is the 2021 edition, published jointly by Jump$tart and the Council for Economic
Education. Your standards crosswalk maps to Jump$tart already, but check the wording on the
site and in `resources/standards-chart-print.html`. If it says "Jump$tart National
Standards" rather than their current title, fix it before you submit. It costs nothing and
a reviewer checking criterion 2 will see their own words looking back.

### ⚠️ Trap 1 — Criterion 8, "broadly available and easily accessible nationwide"
This is the real risk, and it comes straight from the Amazon decision. A reviewer who
expects to find a book on the major retail channel and finds direct-only sales may read
that as narrow availability. Defensible, but you do not want to be arguing it.

**Do not argue it. Route around it.**

**Submit the free educator toolkit as the primary resource, not the book.** The toolkit is
ungated, free, needs no account and no email address, prints from any browser, and is
available to every person in the country with the URL. On criterion 8 it is not merely
compliant — it is the strongest possible answer, and stronger than most of what is already
listed.

Then the storybook goes in as a second listing. By the time that one is reviewed you are
already an approved Provider, and Provider eligibility never has to be argued twice.

### ⚠️ Trap 2 — the commerce exclusion, and the footnote that saves you
Specifically ineligible: resources that "sell or promote products, services, or specific
financial accounts; drive commerce to the resource provider or its affiliates."

On a fast read, a website that sells a book is disqualified. On a correct read, it is not,
because **footnote 1 of their own criteria says so**:

> "Resources are permitted to promote themselves and their related elements. For example, a
> free resource can promote a paid upgrade; a classroom resource can promote its teacher
> training for which there might be a fee."

A free classroom toolkit that mentions the book it accompanies is exactly the example they
wrote down. **Quote footnote 1 in the submission.** Do not wait to be asked and do not make
a reviewer go looking for the exemption on your behalf.

One related note: the book mentions a 529 account. That is a *category* of account defined
in federal tax law, not a specific product from a specific provider, and it is squarely
inside the standards. No exposure.

### ⚠️ Trap 3 — brand names, low risk, have the sentence ready
The story names Xbox, YouTube, and Pokémon, and the glossary defines Black Friday, Cyber
Monday, and Prime Day, describing the last as Amazon's sale event.

None of that promotes a financial product or drives commerce to you, and the three sale
events are taught as consumer-awareness concepts — which is what the standards ask for. Low
risk. But have the sentence ready rather than being surprised by the question.

### ⚠️ Trap 4 — do not submit the wrong things
"Individual articles, blogs, or vlogs" are specifically ineligible. Your LinkedIn posts and
any blog content stay out of this entirely. The toolkit goes in as a collection; the book
goes in as a storybook. Nothing else.

# 7. The parallel track — become a National Partner

Same reference mechanism, bigger prize.

- Request an application, then apply online. It asks about the organization and its
  financial literacy activities and commitment.
- **At least one reference, two better**, ideally from a current National Partner
  organization or an affiliated state coalition.
- Reviewed **quarterly** by the Governance Committee, then a Board of Directors vote.
- Dues are set by type and size of organization. A one-person publisher is the smallest
  category there is — ask the number, do not assume it.
- If you are not accepted, their own guidance says it usually means an organization needs
  more time to establish itself or left something out, and you can work with the Partnership
  Committee on resubmitting.

If this lands, Provider eligibility becomes automatic under route 1 and you never argue it
again. It also puts you in the room with the people whose board seats you have been writing
to one at a time.

**Do both.** The Clearinghouse is faster and needs one letter. The Partner application uses
the same letters and runs on a quarterly clock, so starting it late costs you three months
for nothing.

One more thing worth naming: Delaware's Jump$tart coalition is dormant and the national
contact for restarting it is Variny Yim — that letter is already written in
`letters-delaware.md`. Three separate approaches to the same organization, from three
different directions, none of them through Laura Levine.
