# Competitive Audit: Kids' Financial Literacy Authors & Brands on Social

Compiled June 2026 by a research agent scouring LinkedIn and Facebook for the highest-engagement posts in the category, and decoding what's working vs. what's flatlining.

A note up front: LinkedIn gates most posts behind authentication, so where comment counts couldn't be verified directly, they're flagged "unverified." A lot of the players in this space have surprisingly thin engagement — and that's useful information in its own right.

## Sam X Renick / Sammy Rabbit

**Where active:** LinkedIn (primary), Facebook, YouTube. Sam is the dean of this beat — 20+ years in, 250,000+ kids spoken to, posts almost daily.

**High-engagement post (verified):** His LinkedIn Pulse article "Dad Never Spoke to Me Again" (Sept 2016) opens with his father's deathbed — Sam screaming to wake him, an exchange of "I love you," then silence forever. He pivots from grief to a Cambridge study on money habits forming by age 7. **Verified engagement: 21 likes, 1 comment.** Modest, but the post is nearly a decade old. The hook is what's notable.

**Why it worked:** Raw vulnerability up front, research citation as the payoff. He earned the right to pitch Sammy Rabbit by bleeding first.

**What to steal:** Sam runs a recurring series called "What Was Your First Money Memory?" where he interviews finance pros about ages 6–8 — Sheila reciting poetry for coins, Hugh trading farm eggs, Doug wrapping coins with his family in the 1960s. The series format is the play: a single repeatable question that invites strangers to volunteer specific, sensory childhood scenes. Jonathan could run "Page 22 of Your Childhood" — ask parents to name the moment they realized comparison shopping was real.

# Synthesis: what's actually working in this category

## The 3–5 patterns that pull comments

1. **One specific question, repeated as a series.** Sam Renick's "What was your first money memory?" pulls strangers into the comments because the question is concrete enough that anyone has an answer in 5 seconds. Greenlight ran the same play ("What is one money lesson your parents taught you that stuck?"). The series format compounds — every installment recruits new commenters.

2. **Personal vulnerability before pitch.** Sam's deathbed article got 21 likes in a niche where most posts get 4. Confessional first, lesson second, product third. Skip the order at your peril.

3. **The checklist promise.** Tom Henske's 22-comment post was titled "Everything Your Child Should Know About Money." "Everything" is bold and stupid and works. Parents want the list.

4. **Nostalgia for the parent, not the kid.** Erica Sandberg's "9 Ways Your Parents Saved Money" leans on parents' own childhoods. The parent is the buyer. Speak to her memory, not her kid's future.

5. **Specific dollar amounts, specific page numbers.** Jonathan already does this. "Page 22. Aisle Five." beats "a key scene in the book." Greenlight's "$4.37 saved" beats "significant savings." Specificity is the only reliable engagement hack that doesn't feel like an engagement hack.

## The 3–5 things that flatline

1. **Stats-only brand posts.** Greenlight's 4.3M-games-played post: 55 reactions, 1 comment. Numbers without a person in them die.

2. **Institutional announcements.** CEE's partnership posts with 17K followers pull single-digit comments. Don't write press releases. Write classroom moments.

3. **"Financial Literacy Month" wallpaper.** Every brand posts the same thing every April. Nobody comments on a calendar.

4. **Product-only posts.** Susan Beacham's pig posts that just show the pig don't move. The ones with a kid story attached do.

5. **The "thoughtful resource" tone.** Anything that reads like a grant application back cover. No teacher has ever commented on the word "comprehensive."

## Recommended visual treatments

- **Real classroom photos beat author selfies.** Teachers comment on photos that look like their own rooms.
- **Single-object hero shots.** Susan's pig. Jonathan's neon-orange Clearance sign. One image, recurring, recognizable.
- **Book spreads with a hand or a kid in frame.** Pure spreads feel like Amazon listings. A small hand pointing at "Aisle Five" feels like a moment.
- **Carousels with 1 idea per slide.** LinkedIn rewards carousels right now. 6–8 slides, one concept each, last slide = CTA.
- **Avoid stock imagery and AI illustration.** Both read as low-effort. The book's existing art is the moat — use it.

## What Jonathan owns that nobody else does

1. **The Clarence / Clearance wordplay.** Nobody in this space has a built-in pun moment as a plot device. It's a turning point in the book *and* a teaching unit. Make a recurring social bit out of misreads: "Sale signs my kid misread this week."

2. **Mom photographs every receipt.** That's in the glossary, page 22 callback. It's a real adult money habit hiding inside a kid book. Post it as a parent challenge: "Photograph one receipt this week and tell me what you'd change."

3. **Page 22. Aisle Five.** Nobody else has named a single page of their book as a brand asset. He has. Lean on it harder.

4. **The 30+ years credential on Maryann.** Other authors lean on their own bios. Jonathan has a named veteran educator as his curriculum co-pilot. Most picture books don't.

5. **Wyze as a quiet pun in the glossary itself.** "A certain Wyze kid we know." Inside-baseball wordplay that rewards careful readers. Tease it on socials: "There's a pun hiding in the back of the book. First parent to spot it gets a free toolkit."

6. **The dad-joke energy.** Jonathan's voice — periods as weapons, retail-coupon metaphors — is the differentiator. The category is full of beige "thoughtful resource" copy. He sounds like a person. Most don't.

## What to NOT do

- Don't write a "5 Ways to Teach Your Kid About Money" post. That post has been written 800,000 times.
- Don't lean on "Financial Literacy Month" as a hook. Every April it's wallpaper.
- Don't post emoji-bulleted stat brags. See Greenlight. They have 45K followers and got 1 comment.
- Don't paraphrase the book. Quote it verbatim per Jonathan's voice rules.
